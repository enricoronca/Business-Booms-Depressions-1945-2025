# INFLATION–DEFLATION AREAS

A study of commodity prices across this chart shows that the eighty years since 1945 have had their own sharp disturbances of prices, though they did not follow the pattern of the earlier wars.

This chart does not draw the price level itself, as the 1944 chart did. The dashed line (– – –) shows how much wholesale commodity prices changed over the previous twelve months. Where it runs in the Light Green area, prices were lower than a year earlier. In the Dark Green area prices were rising. The Red area marks the periods of real inflation, when prices ran more than 10 per cent above the year before for at least three months, drawn red down to the base line. The shaded bands under the base line show the years of war.

Prices still answer to supply, demand and cost. Regulation can hold them for a while, as the original chart observed; it cannot abolish those forces. The postwar years tested this twice, in 1946 and in 1971–74, and both times prices rose as soon as the controls were lifted.

A brief comparison of the price reactions since 1945 shows a change: after the Second World War, the large inflations came from oil and from money, and no longer chiefly from war.

**The Postwar Release, 1946–48** — Wartime price controls were lifted in the second half of 1946, while savings accumulated during the war met a short supply of civilian goods. Wholesale prices rose 53 per cent between December 1945 and August 1948, and in March 1947 they stood 35 per cent above a year earlier. The rise ended in 1949, when prices fell nearly 8 per cent in a year during the inventory recession.

**Korea, 1950–51** — When war began in June 1950, buyers expected the shortages of the last war to return and bought ahead. Prices rose 18 per cent in a year by February 1951. A general price freeze followed in January 1951; as fears of shortage faded, the speculative buying stopped and prices slipped back for most of the next four years.

**The Long Calm, 1952–64** — After Korea, wholesale prices hardly moved for twelve years. In December 1964 they stood less than 6 per cent above January 1952, and in no month did they differ from a year earlier by more than 4½ per cent. With no red at all, these are the quietest years of prices on this chart.

**The Inflation of the 1970s** — From 1965 to 1971, the years of heaviest fighting in Vietnam, prices rose slowly, never more than 5 per cent in a year. The great rise came afterwards. The wage and price controls of August 1971 held prices only while they lasted; then the oil embargo of October 1973 and a run-up in food and raw-material prices drove wholesale prices up 38 per cent in two years, to a peak of 23 per cent a year in November 1974. A second oil shock followed the revolution in Iran in 1979, and prices rose 16 per cent a year by early 1980. The rise was broken by very high interest rates set by the Federal Reserve after 1979, at the cost of the two recessions of 1980 and 1981–82 (black area).

**The Quiet Years, 1982–2000** — Once the inflation of the 1970s was broken, prices settled into a long calm. From 1982 to 1999 wholesale prices rose 28 per cent in all, less than 1½ per cent a year, and the short rises around the Gulf War of 1990 and in 2000 stayed well under the red line.

**Falling Prices** — The Light Green areas show that prices still fell, in about one month in four since 1945, though never for long, and not at all in the 1970s. The deepest falls came when oil prices collapsed: in 1986, in 2001–02, in 2015 and in the spring of 2020, each time about 4 to 8 per cent below a year earlier. Only the fall of 2009, at 16 per cent, recalls the deflations that followed the earlier wars, and it lasted a year.

**The Commodity Boom of 2008** — Strong demand from fast-growing economies abroad carried oil and raw materials to record prices. By July 2008 wholesale prices were 17 per cent above a year earlier. The financial crisis reversed this within a few months, and by July 2009 prices were 16 per cent below the year before, the sharpest fall on this chart. A smaller rise, to 11 per cent, followed in 2011.

**Pandemic and War, 2021–22** — Prices fell in the spring of 2020, when the economy closed. When it reopened, demand returned faster than supply chains and shipping could recover. Wholesale prices rose 23 per cent in a year by November 2021, and the invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 pushed energy and food prices higher still. From April 2020 to June 2022 they rose 51 per cent, nearly as much as in 1946–48. Prices then fell back during 2023 and were rising slowly again in 2025.

**The Peaks Compared** — Measured by the yearly change, the sharpest inflation on this chart was the first: 35 per cent in March 1947. The peaks of November 1974 and November 2021 came next, at 23 per cent each, then Korea in February 1951 at 18 per cent, July 2008 at 17 per cent and February 1980 at 16 per cent. The 1970s stand apart by their length rather than their height: from 1973 to 1981 the Red area covers more than four and a half years in two long spells, while every other red area lasted less than two years.

**Wars and Prices** — In the original chart every great price peak stood on top of a war. Here the red areas fall inside the bands of war several times, but only in Korea did the war itself start the rise. The Vietnam War raised prices slowly; the great inflation of 1973–74 came as American forces were leaving, and was driven by oil and food. The peaks of 2008, 2011 and 2021 came during the long wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, but were driven by oil, demand and supply, not by the wars. The war in Ukraine added to a rise that was already under way.

@@ Source of Data: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Producer Price Index by Commodity, All Commodities (PPIACO), via Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED).

# PRICE TRENDS

The white pillars standing in the Red and Dark Green areas show what happened to the buying power of the dollar during each period of inflation. Each pillar shows how many cents’ worth of goods a dollar of the base year bought at the end of the period, measured by the consumer price index for that item.

From 2019 to 2025 the cost-of-living dollar shrank to 80¢. The largest increase was in electricity, where the 2019 dollar bought only 74¢ worth; food followed at 76¢ and shelter at 77¢. Clothing prices rose least: the 2019 dollar still bought 95¢ worth.

The earlier pillars show the same pattern. The 1965 dollar and the 1975 dollar each bought 59¢ worth six to ten years later, and the 2001 dollar bought 82¢ worth in 2008. The one exception on the chart is clothing from 2001 to 2008: with cheap imports and the end of import quotas in 2005, the 2001 dollar bought 107¢ worth of clothing, more than it had at the start.

Over the whole period the change is much larger. The 1947 dollar bought about 7¢ worth of goods in 2025, and the 1965 dollar about 10¢. In 2025 alone the dollar lost about 3¢ of its 2024 value.

@@ Source of Data: U.S. Dept. of Labor, Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer Price Index (all items, food, apparel, shelter, electricity), via FRED.


# NATIONAL INCOME

The National Income (see Green line on chart) for 2025 is estimated at 25.8 trillion dollars, an all-time high and 5.7% more than in 2024. Prices account for a large part of that rise: the economy’s real output grew 2.1% in 2025. Personal income rose 4.9%, and corporate profits by about 276 billion dollars.

In dollars, National Income is about 128 times larger than in 1945. Most of that increase reflects the shrinking dollar shown in the pillars; the rest is real growth in output.

In 1944 the original chart pointed out that the war debt would have to be carried by the National Income of the postwar years. That is what happened at first. The debt was hardly paid down — it was 269 billion dollars in 1946 and 286 billion in 1960 — but income grew so fast around it that by 1950 the debt was smaller than one year’s National Income, and by 1974 it was little more than a third of it. From the 1980s the debt grew faster than income again. In 2010 the two lines crossed (Red line above Green), and in 2025 the debt stands at 1.46 times National Income, higher than at the end of the Second World War.

@@ Source of Data: U.S. Dept. of Commerce, Bureau of Economic Analysis.


# FEDERAL DEBT

The Federal Debt (see Red line on chart) stood at 37.6 trillion dollars at the end of the 2025 fiscal year, on 30 September 2025. Of this, 7.4 trillion dollars is owed by one part of the government to another, chiefly to trust funds such as Social Security. The debt held by the public, 30.3 trillion dollars, is the part the government has borrowed in the markets.

In each year from 2022 to 2025 it grew by more than 2 trillion dollars. Of the present debt, 27.6 trillion dollars, almost three-quarters, was added after 2008.

# BUSINESS ACTIVITY

Industrial Production (Solid Black Areas) is shown as its distance above or below its own ten-year average, the “normal” of the original chart. In 2025 production was about 7 times as large as the average of 1945, but slightly smaller than in 2018, when it reached its highest level. With production flat for seven years, the black area has stayed close to the normal line since the sharp fall of 2020. Because the ten-year average cannot yet be completed for the last five years, the black area after late 2021 (grey band) is provisional and will change as new figures arrive.

**Stocks:** The stock scale follows the Dow Jones Industrial Average of 30 large companies (see CHAIN LINE). The average stood at 154 in January 1945 and at 48,063 at the end of December 2025, the highest month-end level on the chart. Its sharpest falls came in 1973–74, in the crash of October 1987, in 2000–02 and in 2008–09.

**Bonds:** Interest rates have moved through a full cycle since 1945. The yield on long-term government bonds, 2.4% in 1945, rose to 15.8% in September 1981 and fell to 0.6% in July 2020. At the end of 2025 the 10-year Treasury yielded 4.2%, and the highest-grade corporate bonds 5.3%, against 1.94% and 3.1% when the original chart was printed. (See DOTTED LINE; high yields are plotted low.)
